Holidays and birthdays are often a time for creating memories with loved ones. We gather together to share meals, exchange gifts, and enjoy traditions that bring us closer. But for those who are incarcerated, in rehab, or living in group homes, these celebrations can feel emotionally overwhelming and isolating.

Growing up, holidays were filled with music, decorations, and family traditions. I'll never forget the Christmases when my sibling and I would belt out songs from Amy Grant, Faith Hill, Christina Aguilera, and Celine Dion, all while decorating the tree. I'd wear my Santa hat and eagerly unpack the holiday figurines we only saw once a year. One of my favorite traditions was setting up the nativity scene with my sibling and placing each figure carefully on the coffee table. Over the years, I collected Nutcrackers as a nod to my love of *The Nutcracker* ballet, which I'd watch every year on VHS, mesmerized by the dancers even though it had no dialogue.

Birthdays were equally memorable. From the moment I opened my eyes, everything was about me. Whether it was a fancy tea party with my second-grade friends, a sleepover, a trip to Disneyland, or a day trip to San Diego, every detail was chosen with care. The gifts were equally thoughtful — always something I truly wanted, something that would make me feel loved and celebrated.

But all that changed when I ended up in the group homes.

### **My First Holiday Away!**

The first holiday season I spent away from home was the hardest. The anger and frustration I felt were overwhelming. I wasn't ready to be in a group home for Christmas. While we could call our families, I cut my call short — I didn't want to hear their voices, knowing I couldn't be with them. I kept to myself that day, withdrawing as much as possible. My family sent a few gifts — things like a teddy bear, a journal, and some books — and we had a slower-paced day with a special holiday meal. I remember savoring the rolls with extra butter that night. It was a small comfort, but it was all I had.

### **My First Birthday Away, Then a New Bed!**

The next year, I spent my birthday away from my family for the first time. It was nothing like the birthdays I was used to. They sent clothes — pajamas, cozy socks, and other necessities like phone cards and snacks. I thought, at the time, that I'd be going home soon, but I had no idea what was ahead.

I was moved to a new group home just before Christmas, which was even farther away from my family. That year, my birthday felt like another reminder of how far apart we were. Still, I opened my gifts, including a CD player with a few albums from my favorite artists. Music became my escape, my solace in a world that felt increasingly disconnected.

### **My First Snowy Christmas!**

By my third year away, the holidays had become a blur of pain and emptiness. My family was still sending gifts, and I was able to call them for my birthday. But the visits were few and far between, and I was learning how to say goodbye, even though I didn't want to. My inner turmoil only grew as I got news that a family member had attempted to take their own life. I was devastated and reacted violently, running through the halls of the group home, punching walls, and spiraling further into anger.

When I was called to the office, I was informed that my family had made arrangements for me to either be handcuffed in a car for 12 hours or to behave and get on a plane to my next destination. I was furious, but after much contemplation, I chose the plane. It was a small act of defiance, but I was emotionally exhausted by this point.

That Christmas, I experienced my first snowy holiday. I also slipped and landed flat on my butt my first day at the new group home. When I settled into the new facility, I opened my gifts — most of which were considered contraband — and were sent back to my family. At that point, I had no more tears to shed.

### **Tenacious V!**

The following year, my birthday came quickly, and by then, I'd made significant progress in my therapy program. I was even able to call my family and share the news. One of my gifts was a Gameboy Advance — perfect for keeping my mind occupied.

During the Holiday season, I became more involved in helping others who didn't have family visiting, offering support and companionship. We'd play games, listen to music, and share stories, all while silently acknowledging the painful reality of being away during the holidays. The staff prepared a special meal, and while it wasn't the same as being with family, it was a comforting distraction from the loneliness that seemed to define my holidays. When the day ended, I was ready for bed, exhausted from the emotional weight of it all.

### **Graduation and the Journey Home**

By my birthday in 2003, I had made the most progress since my journey began in 1999. But just as I thought I was ready to go home, I was told I'd have to stay for six more months. I lost my level, which sent me to an isolation unit, but I eventually worked my way back up. When my birthday came, I could request an escorted walk outside, and I got a brief but meaningful call from my family.

March came, and I received the best news of all: "Congratulations, Vanessa! You've graduated from high school!" I was relieved, but the transition back home was still ahead. In the meantime, I worked as a teacher's aide, grading papers, collecting homework, and learning how to interact with others in a structured, supportive environment.

Eventually, I earned the highest level back at the group home and was finally ready to go home. Mission accomplished!

### **My First Holiday Back Home**

Returning home after so many years felt like a whirlwind of emotions. I was ecstatic to be back, but there was a deep sense of guilt, knowing that many of my friends were still in group homes. The transition back into society was more complicated than I expected. Even though I was back with my family, we didn't understand how much the adjustment would affect me.

Looking back on this journey, I realize that everything I went through prepared me to help others. I now work to support individuals who are transitioning from incarceration, rehab, and group homes. I've found purpose in helping people break the cycle of recidivism and heal from trauma, just as I did.

### **A Helpful List: Gifts for Loved Ones in Group Homes**

If you have a loved one in a group home, here's a list of thoughtful gifts that might brighten their day:

- **Digital Radios** (with AA batteries and headphones — check with the facility for allowed items)
- **Books** (personal development, religious, autobiographies, etc.)
- **Coloring Books and Crayons/Pencils**
- **Slippers and Cozy Socks**
- **Journals**
- **Magazines**
- **Stickers**
- **Yarn and Crochet Needles**
- **Clothes** (coats, shirts, pants, PJs, hats, beanies, etc.)
- **Handheld Game Consoles** (check facility policies)
- **Phone Cards** (to stay connected with loved ones)

Together, we can **rebel against recidivism**, heal trauma, and reinvent our lives. If you or someone you know could benefit from my experience, please share this blog and contact me!

Here's to your **Rebel Journey**!

Vanessa
